4 Pieces Of Advice For Every Grad Starting Their First Job

ByAndy Molinsky,

Contributor.
Andy Molinsky helps young professionals navigate workplace success.

What you notice in year one matters more than what you accomplish.

You spent four years doing everything right. The internships, the GPA, the career fairs. Now you're graduating and heading into your first job, and here's what nobody told you: your job description matters far less than what you choose to pay attention to.

Most people spend year one with their heads down, mastering the job. The ones who look up are learning something more valuable. Here are four things worth paying attention to in year one.
1. The Best Thing You Can Do Is Say Yes to Things That Scare You

Your first year is not the time to play it safe. Volunteer for the presentation nobody wants to give. Join the project that has nothing to do with your job description. Raise your hand in the meeting even when you're not sure what you're going to say.

The point is exposure. Doing things that push you outside your comfort zone is how you find out what you're good at, what you enjoy, and what kind of work actually suits you. Every time you say yes to something uncomfortable, you learn something about yourself that you couldn't have learned any other way. Year one is the best time to collect that data.
2. Watch the Room Before You Try to Own It

Before you try to make an impression, make some observations. Every workplace is a social system with unwritten rules, hidden hierarchies, and invisible dynamics that most people never notice because they're too busy performing.

Who commands attention when they walk in and why? Whose idea gets shot down at 2pm and credited to someone senior at 4pm? How do people handle disagreement? Ask yourself why, every time. That habit is what separates someone who works at a company from someone who understands it.
3. Your Next Career Might Be Hiding Inside Your Current One

Early in my career, I worked at a small French consulting firm. The actual project work was fine. But something else kept pulling at my attention. I was fascinated by how people communicated across cultural lines. How the French colleagues handled disagreement differently than the Americans. How formality shifted depending on who was in the room. How the same idea landed completely differently depending on who was delivering it.

Nobody asked me to study any of this. No assignment, no grade, no obvious career payoff. I just couldn't stop noticing it. So I started keeping a secret file on my computer, notes about communication patterns, code-switching, the invisible social choreography happening around me every day.

That file sent me to graduate school. It became the foundation of my entire academic career. I had never taken a single course on any of it in college. It came entirely from curiosity and attention.

Your first job is full of those sparks. They might have nothing to do with your title, your industry, or anything you studied. Follow them anyway. You cannot know in advance which rabbit hole leads somewhere extraordinary.
4. Forget Personal Branding. Just Be Reliable

You’ve heard the advice to build your personal brand early. It's good advice. But here's what it usually leaves out: when you're new, there's really only one brand worth having, and it's the same for everyone. Be reliable.

That sounds like table stakes, but a surprising number of people never clear it. Deadlines get blown. Follow-ups get forgotten. Commitments quietly disappear. Do what you say you’ll do and finish what you start. Before senior people care about your ideas or your ambition, they want to know one thing: can I count on you? Answer that convincingly and the rest becomes a lot easier.

Nobody is going to hand you a syllabus for what actually matters in year one. Watch, follow your curiosity, and say yes to things that scare you. That's where the real education begins.
